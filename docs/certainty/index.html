The wager

A procedure guarantees a certain answer to whether God exists — no deception, no error. Immediately afterward, you permanently lose your sight. You gain nothing else: no heaven, money, or longer life. Only the answer. Not one of 300 personas accepted — 94% "probably refuse," 6% "definitely refuse" — and religiosity, the covariate that should matter most, moved nothing: the devout and the atheists refused at identical rates.

The finding: this population isn't refusing certainty — it's refusing blindness specifically. Swap the identical metaphysical payoff to a decade of lifespan and a majority takes the deal. Sight prices out above ten years of life.

Replicate this with real people

These numbers are locked predictions, published before any human data was collected. The unanimous 0% is the most suspicious number here: synthetic respondents share one underlying model, and real populations have tails. If even a few humans in 40 accept the baseline, that gap measures the panel's conformity bias — which is exactly what this study exists to find out. Ask people these questions in this order (rotate 3–7 between respondents):

1. The baseline. A procedure will tell you with absolute certainty whether God exists — no deception or error possible. Immediately afterward, you permanently lose your sight. You gain nothing else — only the answer. Do you accept? (definitely accept / probably accept / probably refuse / definitely refuse) Why?
2. Which is closest to your current belief? (believe God exists / lean toward existing / genuinely unsure / lean toward not existing / believe God does not exist)
3. The procedure only works one way: if God exists you'll know; if not, you learn nothing — and still go blind. Accept?
4. Same deal, but you alone will know, and can never prove it to anyone. Accept?
5. The knowledge fades completely after one year; the blindness is permanent. Accept?
6. Same certain answer, but the price is ten years off the end of your life instead of your sight. Accept?
7. The question answered is instead: “Is there an afterlife?” — at the price of your sight. Accept?
8. Maximum price. Of these — nothing, a finger, your sense of taste, ten years of life, your eyesight, your remaining lifespan — what is the most you would actually pay for the certain answer? Why?

Record: age band, religious tradition, and how important faith is in your life (very / somewhat / not).

Method. 300 synthetic personas (demographics sampled from real Census ACS PUMS records for Spokane and Kootenai counties; religiosity modeled from regional distributions; occupations and life details resolved by an LLM coherence pass), answering in character via claude-sonnet-5 with structured outputs; variant order randomized per persona. Study cost: about $3.50. Known limitations, stated before human data exists: synthetic respondents share one model, so errors correlate — unanimity is evidence of model consensus, not population consensus; and religiosity here is modeled, not sampled from records. The human replication is the point. A TableRead study.
